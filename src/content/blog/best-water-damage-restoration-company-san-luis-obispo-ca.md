## What should you look for in a water damage restoration company?

Look for four things before you let anyone start work: IICRC certification, a real 24/7 answer (not a voicemail), direct insurance billing, and a written scope before demolition begins. These four separate a company that documents a loss properly from one that just shows up with fans.

IICRC certification means the technicians trained on the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage restoration standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), the industry reference for how water losses are categorized, how drying is measured, and when a structure is considered dry. Ask for the certification by name, not just "we're licensed." In California, also confirm the company holds an active contractor license. You can look any license number up yourself on the [CSLB license check tool](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx) before a crew ever reaches your door.

Direct insurance billing matters because most homeowners are filing a claim at the same time water is sitting on their floor. A company that documents moisture readings, photographs the loss, and submits directly to the adjuster saves you from becoming the go-between on paperwork while you're also trying to save your furniture.

## How do you compare water damage restoration estimates?

Compare estimates by what's written in the scope, not by the total dollar figure alone. A lower number that skips moisture mapping or antimicrobial treatment on Category 2 or 3 water isn't actually cheaper; it's a job that gets reopened in three weeks when mold shows up behind the baseboard.

A written scope should include: the water category and class per the IICRC S500 standard, which materials are being dried in place versus removed, the number of air movers and dehumidifiers planned, and an estimated drying timeline with a re-check schedule. ## Will the company bill your insurance directly?

Most established restoration companies will bill your insurer directly for the mitigation portion of a water loss, but confirm it before the crew starts, not after. Direct billing means the company submits its invoice to your adjuster based on the documented scope, rather than requiring you to pay out of pocket and seek reimbursement.

This only works smoothly when the documentation is solid: moisture readings at the start and end of drying, photos of affected materials, and a clear notation of water category and class. Most homeowners insurance policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst supply line or a failed appliance hose, but typically exclude gradual leaks and flood intrusion from outside the home (that needs a separate flood policy). Coverage details vary by insurer and policy, so read your declarations page or ask your agent directly rather than assuming. For a deeper look at what's typically covered, see the [Insurance Information Institute's guide to homeowners coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).
