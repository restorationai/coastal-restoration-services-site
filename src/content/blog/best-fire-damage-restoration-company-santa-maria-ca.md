If a kitchen fire, an electrical short, or a wildfire ember just damaged your Santa Maria home, you're probably getting calls or ads from several restoration companies within hours. That's normal, fire losses move fast and so does the sales outreach around them. What matters now is picking a company that can actually document the loss correctly, stop soot and smoke from spreading further into the structure, and get your insurance claim paid without fights over scope. Here's the checklist to run through before you pick anyone.

## What should you check before hiring a fire damage restoration company in Santa Maria?

Check five things: an active California contractor license, IICRC-trained technicians, direct insurance billing, in-house smoke and contents work, and a written scope of work. A company missing more than one of these is worth skipping, even if they can start today.

Fire restoration isn't like a typical handyman job. It involves structural cleaning to the [ANSI/IICRC S700 fire and smoke standard](https://iicrc.org/s700/), content pack-out and cleaning, odor neutralization that goes well beyond a can of Febreze, and usually a six-figure insurance claim. A company that cuts corners on any of those five items usually shows it in the final result: lingering smoke odor, a claim denial over missing documentation, or a reconstruction delay because nobody wrote down the original scope.

## Is the company licensed and insured to do fire restoration work in California?

Any company doing structural repair, demolition, or reconstruction work in California needs an active license from the Contractors State License Board, and you can look that license up yourself in under a minute. Don't take a license number on faith, run it.

Use the [CSLB license lookup tool](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx) and search the number the company gives you. It should come back active, with no recent disciplinary actions, and the business name should match. Coastal Restoration Services Inc carries California contractor license #11245226, and that number is verifiable the same way. If a company won't give you a license number up front, or gives you one that doesn't match their business name, move on.

## Are the technicians trained to IICRC standards for fire and smoke restoration?

Look for a company that identifies as an IICRC Certified Firm, meaning its technicians are trained against the restoration industry's recognized technical standards rather than figuring it out on the job. That distinction matters most on smoke, because smoke behaves differently depending on what burned.

Protein smoke from a kitchen fire, synthetic smoke from burned plastics and electronics, and wildfire smoke that settled through HVAC returns all require different cleaning agents and sequencing. A crew working from the [IICRC's published standards](https://www.iicrc.org/) knows to test char and residue type before picking a cleaning method, instead of applying the same approach to every job. Ask directly: "Is your firm IICRC certified, and are the technicians on my job trained to that standard?" A straight yes with a certification number is a good sign. A vague answer is not.

## Does the company handle smoke odor removal and contents restoration in-house?

The strongest fire restoration companies handle structural cleaning, [smoke damage restoration](/services/smoke-damage-restoration/), [odor removal](/services/odor-removal/), and [contents restoration](/services/contents-restoration/) under one roof, instead of subcontracting pieces out to three different vendors you never meet.

That matters for two reasons. First, accountability: if the odor comes back three weeks after the job is "done," you want one company responsible for the fix, not a finger-pointing match between a restoration contractor and a cleaning sub. Second, speed: contents that get boxed up and sent to an off-site vendor often sit for weeks before cleaning even starts, which extends your displacement and your claim. Ask whether smoke odor treatment (thermal fogging, hydroxyl or ozone treatment, sealing and painting affected surfaces) and contents pack-out, cleaning, and storage happen under the same company name on your estimate.

## Will the company work directly with your insurance adjuster and give you a written scope?

A reputable fire restoration company documents the loss with photos and moisture or soot readings, writes a line-item scope, and submits it to your adjuster directly, rather than leaving you to translate technical findings into insurance language yourself.

Get the scope in writing before demolition or cleaning begins, not after. This protects you if the adjuster disputes a line item later, and it protects the contractor from scope-creep accusations. If a company wants a verbal go-ahead and a handshake instead of paperwork, that's a red flag regardless of how fast they can show up.
