## What drives the price of storm damage cleanup?

The biggest cost driver is how long water had access to the structure before someone stopped it. A roof breach tarped within hours costs far less to repair than one left open through a full day of rain.

Other factors that move the number:

- **Size of the breach.** A single broken window costs a few hundred dollars to board; a torn-off section of roof decking costs thousands to tarp and repair.
- **Roof pitch and story count.** Steep or multi-story roofs need more rigging, safety equipment, and labor time for tarping.
- **How much water reached the interior.** Once rain gets past the roofline or siding, you're paying for extraction, dehumidification, and drying on top of the exterior repair. We cover that side of the cost in detail in our [water damage restoration cost guide for California](/blog/water-damage-restoration-cost-ca/).
- **Debris volume.** Downed branches, torn fencing, and loose roofing material all add disposal fees.
- **Material exposure.** Drywall, insulation, and flooring soaked by storm water usually need to be removed rather than dried in place once they've sat for more than 24 to 48 hours, per the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for water damage restoration.
- **Secondary damage risk.** Mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, so delayed mitigation adds remediation cost later even if it feels cheaper to wait.

## Does homeowners insurance cover storm damage in California?

Most California homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental damage from wind and wind-driven rain, such as a tree limb breaking a window or a gust tearing off roof shingles and letting rain in. What they typically do not cover is damage from surface flooding, groundwater, or water that rises up from outside the home; that requires a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood carrier.

This distinction matters because rain coming through a storm-damaged roof usually starts as relatively clean water, but it can become contaminated the longer it sits in contact with insulation, ceiling material, and HVAC ductwork. Rising floodwater or surface water flowing into a home from outside is treated differently and needs documentation that a storm claim alone won't provide. The [California Department of Insurance's residential claims guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/03-res/) walks through how claims are typically filed and what documentation insurers expect. Our team also put together a [plain-English guide to water damage insurance claims](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) if you want the broader rules before you call your carrier.

Documentation protects your claim either way. Photograph the damage before anything is moved, keep every receipt for emergency mitigation (tarping, board-up, extraction), and get a written scope of work. Most policies also require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, which is exactly what emergency board-up and tarping are for.

## What happens during a storm damage cleanup?

Storm cleanup moves in a set sequence: stop the intrusion first, document everything, then dry and repair. Skipping or reordering these steps is what usually turns a $3,000 job into a $15,000 one.

1. **Emergency mitigation.** Crews install [tarping or board-up](/services/emergency-board-up-tarping/) to stop further wind and rain from entering the structure. This happens before any cost discussion about the full repair, because every hour of continued exposure adds damage.
2. **Damage assessment and documentation.** A technician photographs and measures the affected area, checks moisture levels with a meter, and notes which materials are salvageable.
3. **Water extraction and drying,** if interior rooms took on water. This follows the same drying protocols used for any [flood damage restoration](/services/flood-damage-restoration/) job: extraction equipment, air movers, and dehumidifiers set to dry the structure to pre-loss moisture levels.
4. **Debris removal.** Downed branches, torn roofing material, and damaged fencing are cleared and hauled off.
5. **Insurance documentation and written scope.** The full damage report, photos, and a line-item scope of work go to your adjuster, supporting the claim rather than slowing it down.
6. **Repair and reconstruction scheduling.** Roofing, drywall, and siding repairs follow once the structure is dry and documented.

If you're still monitoring an active storm, the [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/) is the most reliable source for local warnings and rainfall totals, which can also support your claim timeline.
